## 1. Purpose and scope

### 1.1 Purpose

This policy sets out the Trust's expected standard of care for adults (18 years and over) at risk of, or with, delirium. It aims to:

- ensure that delirium is **anticipated, prevented where possible, recognised early and treated as a medical emergency**;
- standardise assessment using the **4AT** (or CAM-ICU / ICDSC in critical care);
- embed the NICE **multicomponent prevention package**;
- make clear that **medication is for severe behavioural disturbance, not for delirium itself**, and set safe, conservative prescribing limits when it is needed;
- ensure care is lawful under the **Mental Capacity Act 2005 (MCA)** and uses the **least restrictive option**;
- ensure patients and families are **informed and involved**, and that a delirium diagnosis is **communicated to the GP** and followed up.

### 1.2 Scope

Applies to all clinical staff (medical, nursing, pharmacy, allied health, healthcare support workers, security staff working clinically) in all adult inpatient areas, the Emergency Department, Same Day Emergency Care, critical care, theatres/recovery and Trust-run long-term care settings.

**Outside scope:** children and young people under 18; delirium caused *solely* by alcohol or substance withdrawal (managed under the Trust Alcohol Withdrawal Guideline / NICE CG100 – see section 13.3), and the management of behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia in the absence of delirium (NICE NG97 – see section 13.1).

## 2. Key messages

1. **Think delirium.** Anyone aged 65+, with cognitive impairment or dementia, with a current hip fracture, or with severe illness is at risk. Assess risk within 24 hours of admission and observe for change at every opportunity (NICE CG103 1.1–1.2).
2. **Delirium is a medical emergency.** It is associated with increased mortality, longer length of stay, institutionalisation and incident dementia. Look for and treat the cause.
3. **Hypoactive delirium is the most commonly missed.** A quiet, withdrawn, sleepy patient who is not eating or drinking may be delirious.
4. **Use the 4AT.** A score of **4 or more** indicates probable delirium; **1–3** indicates possible cognitive impairment; **0** makes delirium unlikely (though it does not exclude it).
5. **If you cannot tell whether it is delirium, dementia or both – manage as delirium first** (NICE CG103 1.6.3; NICE NG97).
6. **Prevent it.** A tailored multicomponent package (orientation, hydration, nutrition, mobilisation, sleep, sensory aids, pain control, medication review, avoiding bed moves) reduces the incidence of delirium by roughly a quarter to a third.
7. **Medication does not treat delirium.** Pharmacological treatment should not be used to treat delirium itself. Medication should only be considered where non-pharmacological measures have been ineffective or are not feasible, **and** the patient's distress or behaviour presents an immediate risk or prevents essential treatment.
8. **If medication is needed:** oral first; haloperidol at the lowest clinically appropriate dose (**0.25–0.5 mg**), repeat only after reassessment, **cumulative dose above 2 mg/24 h in older adults requires senior review**, for the shortest possible time (usually ≤ 1 week), and **never in Parkinson's disease or dementia with Lewy bodies**. Benzodiazepines are not recommended for routine management.
9. **Reassess daily** and stop as soon as clinically appropriate. Antipsychotics started for delirium should not routinely be continued at discharge.
10. **Involve families and carers** and give them written information. Communicate the diagnosis to the GP at discharge (NICE QS63 statements 4 and 5).

## 3. Definitions

| Term | Definition used in this policy |
|---|---|
| **Delirium** | An acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness with a change in cognition (memory, orientation, language, perception), developing over hours to days, and usually caused by an underlying medical condition, substance intoxication/withdrawal, medication, or a combination. Also called "acute confusional state". |
| **Hyperactive delirium** | Heightened arousal – the person may be restless, agitated, aggressive, wandering, pulling at lines. |
| **Hypoactive delirium** | Withdrawn, quiet, sleepy, slow to respond, reduced mobility, reduced oral intake. Frequently missed or misattributed to low mood, tiredness or dementia. |
| **Mixed delirium** | Fluctuation between hyperactive and hypoactive states, often within the same day. |
| **Dementia** | A chronic, progressive, largely irreversible decline in cognition (memory, language, visuospatial and executive function) with preserved attention in the early stages, developing over months to years. |
| **Delirium superimposed on dementia (DSD)** | Delirium occurring in a person with pre-existing dementia; the commonest scenario in older inpatients and the hardest to recognise. |
| **Severe behavioural disturbance** | Behaviour arising from delirium that places the patient or others at immediate risk of physical harm, causes severe distress, or prevents delivery of essential investigation or treatment that cannot safely be delayed. |
| **De-escalation** | Verbal and non-verbal techniques to reduce distress and arousal (calm tone, reassurance, orientation, reducing stimulation, meeting needs such as pain, toileting, thirst). |
| **Least restrictive option** | The MCA principle that any act done for a person who lacks capacity must be the option that least restricts their rights and freedom of action while still achieving the purpose. |
| **Health and social care practitioners** | All staff involved in patient care (nurses, HCAs, therapists, social workers, doctors, pharmacists etc.) – terminology as in NICE CG103. |

## 4. Why delirium matters – the UK context

- Delirium affects around **20–30 % of adult medical inpatients**, up to **50 % of people with hip fracture**, and up to **75 % of patients in intensive care** (NICE CG103; SIGN 157). Prevalence in people with dementia in hospital is considerably higher.
- It is **independently associated with increased mortality, longer length of stay, higher rates of new institutionalisation, falls, pressure injury, hospital-acquired infection and subsequent dementia** (NICE CG103 1.1).
- It is **distressing** – for the patient (who may later recall frightening experiences), for families, and for staff.
- It is **frequently missed**: studies consistently show that a large proportion of delirium in UK hospitals, particularly hypoactive delirium and delirium superimposed on dementia, is not recognised or documented.
- It is **partly preventable**: multicomponent non-pharmacological programmes reduce incident delirium by around 27 % (pooled relative risk ≈ 0.73; SIGN 157).
- **National levers.** NICE CG103 (updated 2023), NICE QS63, SIGN 157, the Royal College of Physicians' NEWS2 (in which *new confusion* scores 3 points under "C" of ACVPU and triggers escalation), the MHRA Drug Safety Update on haloperidol (2021), and NHS England's emergency-admission and hip-fracture standards (which name the 4AT) all set expectations that this policy operationalises.

## 6. Risk factor assessment on admission

**Within 24 hours of admission** (and at pre-operative assessment for elective surgery) every adult must be assessed for the four NICE risk factors (CG103 1.2.1):

1. **Age 65 years or older**
2. **Cognitive impairment (past or present) and/or dementia** – if in doubt, use a validated measure (e.g. AMTS, MoCA) and ask the family/carer about baseline function
3. **Current hip fracture**
4. **Severe illness** (a clinical condition that is deteriorating or at risk of deterioration)

**If any one of these is present, the person is at risk of delirium** and must:

- have a baseline **4AT** recorded (section 9);
- have the **multicomponent prevention package** started within 24 hours (section 7);
- have a **medication review** by an experienced healthcare professional (SIGN 157);
- be **observed at every opportunity** for changes in risk factors or indicators (CG103 1.2.2, 1.5.1).

Additional recognised precipitating and predisposing factors that should heighten vigilance (from the Trust teaching material and SIGN 157): frailty; sensory impairment (vision, hearing); polypharmacy (particularly anticholinergics, opioids, benzodiazepines); infection; dehydration; malnutrition; constipation; urinary retention; pain; sleep deprivation; alcohol misuse; previous delirium; depression; multiple bed moves.

## 7. Prevention – multicomponent intervention

Delivered by a **multidisciplinary team trained and competent in delirium prevention**, tailored to the individual's needs and care setting, and started **within 24 hours of admission** (NICE CG103 1.4). Ensure the same team cares for the person where possible and **avoid moving people within and between wards or rooms unless absolutely necessary** (CG103 1.4.1–1.4.2).

| Domain | Actions (NICE CG103 1.4.3–1.4.10; SIGN 157) |
|---|---|
| **Cognitive impairment / disorientation** | Appropriate lighting and clear signage; a clock (24-hour in critical care) and calendar visible; re-orientate – explain where they are, who you are, what your role is; cognitively stimulating activities (e.g. reminiscence); **facilitate regular visits from family and friends** (open/extended visiting for carers). |
| **Dehydration / constipation** | Encourage oral fluids; fluid balance chart; consider subcutaneous or IV fluids if necessary; take advice on fluid balance in comorbidities (e.g. heart failure, CKD); bowel chart and management of constipation. |
| **Hypoxia** | Assess for hypoxia; optimise oxygen saturation if clinically appropriate. |
| **Infection** | Look for and treat infection; avoid unnecessary catheterisation; infection-control procedures. |
| **Immobility / limited mobility** | Mobilise soon after surgery/admission; encourage walking (with walking aids where needed and accessible); range-of-motion exercises for those who cannot walk. |
| **Pain** | Assess for pain – **look for non-verbal signs**, particularly in people who cannot communicate (e.g. Abbey Pain Scale, PAINAD); start and review appropriate pain management. |
| **Medication** | Review all medicines for type and number; rationalise; minimise anticholinergic burden (see 10.3), opioids, benzodiazepines and other sedatives; check for withdrawal risk. |
| **Poor nutrition** | Follow the Trust nutrition policy; ensure dentures fit properly and are used; assistance with meals; red trays / protected mealtimes. |
| **Sensory impairment** | Resolve reversible causes (e.g. impacted ear wax); ensure **hearing aids and glasses are available, working and worn**. |
| **Sleep disturbance** | Avoid nursing or medical procedures during sleeping hours if possible; schedule medication rounds to avoid disturbing sleep; reduce noise and light at night; avoid sedative-hypnotics. |
| **Bladder and bowel** | Regular toileting; avoid catheters where possible; screen for retention. |

**Antipsychotics and other drugs must not be used prophylactically to prevent delirium** (SIGN 157 – insufficient evidence of benefit; benzodiazepines markedly increase the odds of delirium).

Consider the use of a structured ward-level prevention bundle (e.g. the NHS Scotland **TIME** bundle – *Think/exclude triggers, Investigate and intervene, Management plan, Engage and explore* – or an equivalent Trust care plan) **[Trust to confirm local bundle]**.

## 8. Recognition – indicators and daily observation

### 8.1 Indicators at presentation (NICE CG103 1.3.1 [2023])

Assess for any **recent (hours to days) change or fluctuation** in behaviour, reported by the patient, carer or relative, including:

- **Cognitive function** – worsened concentration, slow responses, confusion, disorientation
- **Perception** – visual or auditory hallucinations, misinterpretations, paranoid ideas
- **Physical function** – reduced mobility, reduced movement, restlessness, agitation, changes in appetite, sleep disturbance
- **Social behaviour** – lack of co-operation with reasonable requests, withdrawal, altered communication, mood or attitude

**Be particularly vigilant for the indicators of hypoactive delirium – withdrawal, slow responses, reduced mobility, reduced oral intake, drowsiness – which are often missed.** "They're just tired / sleepy / a bit low" should prompt a 4AT.

### 8.2 Daily observation (NICE CG103 1.5.1)

Observe **at least daily** all people in hospital or long-term care for recent changes or fluctuations in usual behaviour. Any change reported by a carer, relative or staff member must be documented and acted on – complete a 4AT and inform the responsible clinician.

### 8.3 Triggers that must prompt a 4AT

- Admission of any at-risk patient (section 6)
- **NEWS2 "C – new confusion"** (scores 3; triggers urgent clinical review)
- Any of the indicators in 8.1
- Fall, new incontinence, refusal of food/fluids/medication, new drowsiness
- Post-operatively (recovery and day 1–3), and on transfer from critical care
- A family member saying "this is not like them"
- Any behavioural incident, before medication is considered

## 9. Assessment and diagnosis

### 9.1 Immediate safety – ABCDE

An acute change in cognition is a **medical emergency** until proven otherwise. Before or alongside cognitive testing, complete an **ABCDE assessment**: observations (NEWS2), **capillary glucose**, oxygen saturation, pain assessment, bladder scan for retention, bowel chart, medication chart review and a drug/alcohol history (Trust teaching; SIGN 157 – *first consider acute life-threatening causes: hypoxia, hypotension, hypoglycaemia, intoxication or withdrawal*).

### 9.2 Assessment tool – the 4AT (NICE CG103 1.6.1 [2023]; SIGN 157)

- Use the **4AT** in general hospital wards, the Emergency Department, SDEC and long-term care. It takes under 2 minutes, needs no special training, is free to use, and includes brief cognitive testing (Appendix A).
- In **critical care or post-operative recovery**, use the **CAM-ICU** or the **Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist (ICDSC)** instead (CG103 1.6.1).
- Interpretation: **≥ 4 = probable delirium (± cognitive impairment)**; **1–3 = possible cognitive impairment, delirium not excluded**; **0 = delirium unlikely** (but does not exclude it if the history is suggestive).
- Record the score and date/time in the notes and on the electronic record **[Trust to confirm EPR field]**.

### 9.3 Making the diagnosis (CG103 1.6.2–1.6.5)

- A **healthcare professional trained and competent in diagnosing delirium** should carry out (or confirm) the clinical assessment and make the final diagnosis; this may be the same person who did the 4AT.
- The diagnosis is clinical: acute onset and fluctuating course + inattention + altered level of consciousness or disorganised thinking (the CAM/DSM-5 construct). The 4AT supports but does not replace clinical judgement.
- **If it is difficult to distinguish delirium, dementia or delirium superimposed on dementia, manage as delirium first.**
- **Document the diagnosis of delirium** clearly in the medical record (problem list) and ensure it appears in the discharge summary to primary care (section 16).

### 9.4 Collateral history and baseline

Establish the person's **baseline** cognition and function from a relative, carer, care home, GP record or "This is Me" / carer's passport document. Key questions (Trust teaching): *When did the change start? What is normal for them? Has anything changed – medicines, bowels, sleep, intake, environment?*

### 9.5 Distinguishing delirium from dementia

Delirium is **acute** (hours–days), **fluctuating**, with **impaired attention early**, altered arousal and frequent hallucinations, and is **often reversible**. Dementia is **chronic** (months–years), **progressive**, with **preserved attention early**, and is largely irreversible. See Appendix C. Structured tools such as the long CAM or the Observational Scale of Level of Arousal (OSLA) may help, but **a new diagnosis of dementia should not be made during an episode of delirium** (NICE NG97). Dementia with Lewy bodies (fluctuation, visual hallucinations, parkinsonism, REM sleep behaviour disorder) can mimic delirium – and is the single most important diagnosis to identify before any antipsychotic is considered.

### 9.6 Risk assessment

Document and plan for specific risks (Trust teaching): wandering/absconding; falls; self-harm; aggression towards staff or other patients; removal of cannulas, feeding tubes, catheters, oxygen; sexual disinhibition; damage to property; refusal of essential treatment; dehydration/malnutrition (hypoactive). Complete the falls risk assessment and consider enhanced observation / cohort nursing **before** medication.

### 9.7 Mental capacity

Assess and **document decision-specific capacity** for key decisions (investigations, treatment, remaining in hospital) as soon as practicable. Capacity fluctuates in delirium and must be reassessed. See section 14.

## 10. Identifying and treating the cause

### 10.1 Approach

Delirium is almost always multifactorial. **Identify and manage all possible underlying causes or combinations of causes** (NICE CG103 1.7.1). Use the Trust teaching mnemonic **DELIBERATE** or **PINCH ME** (Appendix B) as a checklist.

### 10.2 Minimum investigation set

| Bedside | Bloods | Other, as indicated |
|---|---|---|
| Full observations (NEWS2), capillary glucose, SpO₂ | FBC, U&E, LFT, bone profile (calcium), magnesium, glucose, CRP, TFT, B12/folate | Urinalysis **only** to support a clinical picture of UTI – a positive dipstick alone in an older person is *not* diagnostic (asymptomatic bacteriuria is common); MSU if symptomatic |
| Bladder scan (retention) | Blood cultures if sepsis suspected | Chest X-ray if respiratory signs |
| Bowel chart / PR if constipation suspected | Drug levels where relevant (e.g. lithium, digoxin, phenytoin) | ECG (also required before any antipsychotic – see 12.6) |
| Pain assessment (incl. non-verbal scale) | Blood gas if hypoxia / hypercapnia suspected | CT head if focal neurology, head injury, on anticoagulants, fall with head strike, or no cause found |
| Hydration / nutrition (MUST) | | EEG if non-convulsive status considered; LP if CNS infection suspected |
| Sensory check (ears, glasses) | | Alcohol/drug history; withdrawal scoring |

### 10.3 Medication review

Review the whole drug chart with pharmacy. Particular culprits: **anticholinergics** (e.g. oxybutynin, tolterodine, tricyclics, hyoscine, chlorphenamine, cyclizine, promethazine), **opioids** (especially tramadol, codeine accumulation in renal impairment), **benzodiazepines and Z-drugs** (intoxication *and* withdrawal), gabapentinoids, corticosteroids, antihistamines, antiparkinsonian agents, digoxin, lithium, and recently stopped medicines (withdrawal). Use an anticholinergic burden scale **[Trust to confirm which – e.g. ACB scale]**. Where medication is required for behavioural disturbance, **choose agents with the lowest appropriate anticholinergic burden** (SG feedback).

### 10.4 Treat what you find

Sepsis pathway; correct hypoxia, hypotension, dehydration, electrolyte disturbance (Na⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺); manage pain; relieve retention and constipation; thiamine (Pabrinex) if alcohol misuse or malnutrition; manage withdrawal (section 13.3); stop or reduce culprit drugs; treat the medical/surgical condition.

## 11. Non-pharmacological management

Non-pharmacological care is **the treatment of delirium**. It is delivered by the whole team, all day and all night, and continues even if medication becomes necessary.

### 11.1 Core measures (NICE CG103 1.7.2; SIGN 157; Trust teaching)

- **Effective communication and reorientation** – introduce yourself each time; explain where they are and why; short simple sentences; one person speaking at a time; visible clock, calendar, whiteboard with the date and named nurse.
- **Reassurance** – acknowledge distress; do not argue with hallucinations or delusions; validate the feeling, redirect gently.
- **Involve family, friends and carers** – they are the best reorientation tool. Apply open/extended visiting for carers of people with delirium or dementia (e.g. John's Campaign principles) **[Trust to confirm carer visiting policy]**; encourage familiar objects, photographs, music.
- **Suitable care environment** – side room or bay by the nurses' station according to need; adequate daytime light, reduced night noise and light; avoid bed/ward moves; consistent staff.
- **Sensory** – glasses, hearing aids (batteries!), dentures.
- **Physiological needs** – hydration, nutrition (assist with meals), toileting, pain relief, sleep.
- **Mobilise** – early, with supervision; avoid unnecessary lines, catheters and restrictions that provoke agitation.
- **Avoid physical restraints**, cot sides used as restraint, and "specialing" that is purely custodial – use engagement-based enhanced observation instead.
- **Manage the environment for hypoactive delirium** – active encouragement of intake, mobilisation and interaction; do not leave the "quiet" patient alone.

### 11.2 De-escalation of distress (NICE CG103 1.7.3; NICE NG10)

If a person with delirium is **distressed or considered a risk to themselves or others**, staff must **first use verbal and non-verbal de-escalation techniques**:

1. **Look for an unmet need or a physical cause** – pain, thirst, hunger, full bladder/bowels, cold/hot, fear, hypoxia, hypoglycaemia, a new acute problem.
2. Approach calmly from the front, at eye level, one person talking, use their name, a soft voice, open body language, no sudden movements.
3. Reduce stimulation – noise, crowding, alarms, bright lights; remove the audience.
4. Reorientate and reassure; offer choices; involve a family member if available (by telephone/video if not).
5. Distract and redirect – a drink, a walk, an activity.
6. Do not confront, restrain, or "correct" delusional beliefs.
7. Give space and time; re-approach later if safe to do so.

Document what was tried and the response. **Only if these are ineffective or inappropriate** (e.g. immediate serious risk) should medication be considered (section 12) – this is the standard set by NICE QS63 statement 3.

## 12. Pharmacological management of severe behavioural disturbance

> This section replaces the previous *Pharmacological management – Sedation treatment* section in full and incorporates all points of the SG feedback. The deliberate change of heading reflects the intent: **the aim is to reduce distress and manage immediate risk, not to sedate the patient.**

### 12.1 Principles

- **Pharmacological treatment should not be used to treat delirium itself.** No drug is licensed to treat delirium in the UK, and antipsychotics have not been shown to reduce delirium severity, duration or mortality (SIGN 157, Cochrane). Medication should only be considered where non-pharmacological measures have been ineffective or are not feasible, and the patient's distress or behaviour presents an immediate risk or prevents essential treatment.
- NICE recommends managing underlying causes and using environmental/non-pharmacological interventions **before** considering medication (CG103 1.7.1–1.7.3).
- **The aim of any medication is behavioural control sufficient to permit safe care and to reduce distress – rather than sedation per se.**
- **Oral administration must be offered where feasible** as the least restrictive approach. Intramuscular administration is reserved for situations where oral medication is not possible or would be ineffective. Intravenous haloperidol is **not** used outside critical care **[Trust to confirm]**.
- Use the **lowest clinically appropriate dose, titrate cautiously, use the minimum effective dose, for the shortest possible time** (NICE CG103 1.7.4; MHRA 2021).
- **Where there is an immediate and serious risk of harm, urgent pharmacological intervention should not be delayed solely to obtain an ECG or because other non-pharmacological measures cannot be completed.** Document the reasoning.
- Where medication is required, **choose agents with the lowest appropriate anticholinergic burden** and review the patient's existing medicines for drugs that may be contributing to the delirium.
- Every dose must be **prescribed** (no verbal orders except in an emergency, confirmed in writing as soon as possible), with the **indication documented**, and given by staff competent in monitoring (12.9).

### 12.2 Indications

Medication should only be considered **after non-pharmacological measures have failed** (or where delay would place the patient or others at immediate risk) **and** the patient is:

- **severely distressed** due to delirium; **or**
- **posing an immediate risk of harm to themselves or others** (behaviour places them or others at immediate risk of physical harm); **or**
- **preventing delivery of essential investigation or treatment that cannot safely be delayed.**

> **Not an indication:** "risk of falls due to agitation". Falls are rarely, if ever, prevented by sedation, and antipsychotics and benzodiazepines themselves **increase** the risk of falls. Manage falls risk through the falls policy, enhanced observation and environment.
> **Not an indication:** wandering, calling out, refusing care that can wait, staff convenience, night-time restlessness alone, or hypoactive delirium.

### 12.3 Before prescribing – checklist

- [ ] Non-pharmacological measures and de-escalation tried and documented (or reason why not feasible)
- [ ] Reversible causes sought (pain, retention, constipation, hypoxia, hypoglycaemia, withdrawal)
- [ ] **Parkinson's disease, dementia with Lewy bodies, parkinsonism** – excluded (if present → **do not use haloperidol**; see 12.5)
- [ ] Recent ECG reviewed or performed where feasible; QTc noted; electrolytes (K⁺, Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺) checked and corrected (12.6)
- [ ] Other QT-prolonging drugs identified (e.g. citalopram/escitalopram, macrolides, quinolones, ondansetron, methadone, amiodarone, domperidone, tricyclics, other antipsychotics)
- [ ] Capacity assessed and documented; best-interests decision recorded if the patient lacks capacity (section 14)
- [ ] Route – oral offered first
- [ ] Indication and target behaviour documented in the notes and on the prescription
- [ ] Monitoring plan in place (12.9)

### 12.4 First line – haloperidol (where no contraindication)

NICE does not recommend one antipsychotic over another. Haloperidol is generally the preferred first-line antipsychotic where there are no contraindications because of its relatively low anticholinergic burden and limited hypotensive effects, and because it is the agent NICE names (CG103 1.7.4: *consider short-term haloperidol, usually for 1 week or less*). However, antipsychotic choice should be tailored to the patient according to comorbidities, side-effect profile and available routes of administration.

| Parameter | Trust standard |
|---|---|
| **Starting dose** | **0.25–0.5 mg** orally (liquid or tablet) or intramuscularly. Use the lower end in frailty, low body weight, age > 80, hepatic impairment. |
| **Route** | **Oral first** wherever feasible. IM only where oral is not possible or would be ineffective. Not IV outside critical care. |
| **Repeat dosing** | **Repeat only after reassessment of mental state, physical observations and adverse effects – usually after 30–60 minutes if clinically indicated.** Do not repeat "until sedation achieved". The Maudsley guidelines are cautious about repeated dosing in frail older adults. |
| **Maximum** | **For older adults (65+) or frail patients: avoid cumulative doses above 2 mg in 24 hours without senior review (registrar or consultant).** For younger, non-frail adults, doses above 2 mg/24 h also require senior review; the Trust does not endorse routine use above 5 mg/24 h **[Trust to confirm]**. |
| **Duration** | Short-term only – **usually 1 week or less** (NICE). Review the indication **at least daily** (12.10). |
| **Anticipated use beyond 48 hours** | **Consider consultation with the Care of the Elderly consultant or Liaison Psychiatry.** |
| **Regular prescribing** | Regular (rather than "as required") prescribing is discouraged; if used, it must have a stop/review date and be reviewed daily. "PRN" prescriptions must state indication, minimum interval and 24-hour maximum. |

Product information for haloperidol permits higher doses in the general adult population; the Trust limits above are **deliberately conservative** in line with the MHRA's advice that *special caution is required when using haloperidol for the acute treatment of delirium in frail, elderly patients* and with the Maudsley Prescribing Guidelines (15th edition).

### 12.5 Contraindications and cautions for haloperidol

**Avoid haloperidol in:**

- **Parkinson's disease** (including drug-induced parkinsonism and Parkinson's disease dementia)
- **Dementia with Lewy bodies** (risk of severe neuroleptic sensitivity reactions – rigidity, immobility, reduced consciousness, NMS-like picture – and worsening parkinsonism; can be fatal)
- **Known prolonged QT interval** or congenital long-QT syndrome
- **Previous neuroleptic malignant syndrome**
- **Significant ventricular arrhythmias** (or history of torsades de pointes)
- **Recent myocardial infarction** / uncompensated heart failure
- Comatose or CNS-depressed states

**Use with particular caution in:** epilepsy (lowers seizure threshold); hepatic impairment; hypotension; concomitant QT-prolonging drugs; electrolyte disturbance; dementia of any type (MHRA: increased mortality and stroke in older people with dementia treated with antipsychotics – NICE nevertheless allows short-term use for severe distress/risk); dysphagia (antipsychotic-induced swallowing difficulty and aspiration risk).

**Adverse effects to watch for:** acute dystonia (can occur after a single dose; treat with procyclidine 5–10 mg IM/IV), akathisia (often mistaken for worsening agitation – do not respond by giving more antipsychotic), parkinsonism, oversedation, hypotension, QT prolongation/arrhythmia, NMS (fever, rigidity, autonomic instability, raised CK), dysphagia and aspiration.

### 12.6 ECG and QTc

- **Wherever feasible, review a recent ECG or perform an ECG before prescribing haloperidol**, particularly in patients with known cardiovascular disease, electrolyte abnormalities, or other QT-prolonging medications (MHRA 2021: a baseline ECG is recommended; NICE NG10: do not use IM haloperidol without an ECG unless the risk justifies it).
- **Correct reversible risk factors – hypokalaemia, hypomagnesaemia and hypocalcaemia – where possible before prescribing haloperidol** (Maudsley emphasises this repeatedly).
- NICE CG103 does not specify a numerical QTc threshold. The Trust adopts the widely used thresholds below **[Trust Medicines Management to confirm]**:

| QTc (Bazett or Fridericia – state which) | Interpretation and action |
|---|---|
| **> 450 ms in men** / **> 460 ms in women** | Prolonged. Seek senior or specialist advice; consider an alternative agent; correct electrolytes; review other QT-prolonging drugs; if haloperidol still used, repeat ECG after dosing. |
| **> 500 ms** (either sex) | **Significantly increased risk of torsades de pointes.** Do not give haloperidol (or other QT-prolonging antipsychotics). Discuss with senior clinician / cardiology / liaison psychiatry. Consider a benzodiazepine if medication is unavoidable (12.8). |
| Increase of > 60 ms from baseline | Treat as significant – stop and review. |

- **If the QTc is prolonged or there is concern regarding arrhythmia risk, seek senior or specialist advice and consider an alternative agent.**
- Repeat the ECG after dose escalation, if cumulative dose exceeds 2 mg/24 h, or if there is any clinical concern. In critical care, continuous cardiac monitoring is used.
- **Immediate risk:** where an ECG cannot be obtained without unacceptable delay in a situation of immediate serious risk, medication may be given and the ECG obtained as soon as practicable afterwards; document the reasoning.

### 12.7 Alternative antipsychotics

NICE doesn't recommend one antipsychotic over another. Choice should be tailored to comorbidities, side-effect profile and available routes. **Seek specialist advice from Liaison Psychiatry regarding alternative medication where haloperidol is contraindicated.** Indicative starting doses below are for older/frail adults and must be confirmed against the BNF/Maudsley with pharmacy **[Trust to confirm doses]**:

| Agent | When it might be preferred | Indicative starting dose (older adult) | Cautions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Olanzapine** | Where additional calming is desirable, or extrapyramidal risk needs to be lower; oral, orodispersible (velotab) and IM available | 2.5 mg orally; repeat after reassessment; usual max 10 mg/24 h in older adults | Anticholinergic; hypotension; **IM olanzapine must not be given within 1 hour of IM/IV benzodiazepine** (respiratory depression); metabolic effects with longer use; same MHRA dementia warning |
| **Quetiapine** | **Parkinson's disease / dementia with Lewy bodies, after specialist advice** (least likely to worsen motor symptoms) | 12.5–25 mg orally | Sedation, orthostatic hypotension, QT prolongation; oral only |
| **Risperidone** | Oral alternative with some evidence in delirium and licensed for short-term BPSD in Alzheimer's | 0.25–0.5 mg orally | EPS at higher dose; hypotension; cerebrovascular risk in dementia |
| **Aripiprazole** | Less metabolic and QT burden; limited evidence in delirium | 5 mg orally (lower in frailty) | Akathisia; delayed onset; limited data |
| **Lorazepam** | Where antipsychotics are contraindicated or high-risk – see 12.8 | 0.5 mg | See 12.8 |

Antipsychotics should **not** be combined with each other. Only one antipsychotic and (exceptionally) one benzodiazepine should be in use at any time.

### 12.8 Benzodiazepines

**Benzodiazepines are not recommended for routine management of delirium** because they may worsen confusion, increase falls risk, cause respiratory depression and prolong delirium (SIGN 157: benzodiazepines markedly increase the odds of delirium; no evidence of benefit in treatment).

**They should generally be reserved for:**

- **alcohol withdrawal delirium** (delirium tremens – see section 13.3);
- **benzodiazepine withdrawal**;
- **where specifically advised by an appropriate specialist** (e.g. palliative care for terminal agitation, liaison psychiatry, neurology for seizure-related states, critical care).

**In exceptional circumstances**, where severe agitation presents an immediate risk **and** antipsychotics are contraindicated or considered high risk (e.g. Parkinson's disease/DLB, QTc > 500 ms, previous NMS, recent MI), **an individualised decision to use a benzodiazepine may be appropriate following senior clinical review.** Pragmatism and patient-tailored choice should carry weight: for example, if urgent treatment of severe psychomotor agitation is required in a patient with diabetes, significant cardiac history and parkinsonism, a benzodiazepine may be the safer choice for that person in that moment. The choice of drug category, and of drug within that category, should be tailored to the patient's history and acuity.

**Lorazepam – Trust standard**

| Parameter | Standard |
|---|---|
| Dose | **0.5 mg initially** (particularly in older adults or frailty), **PO or IM**; increase only if clinically necessary after reassessment |
| Route | **Oral should be offered in the first instance**; IM where oral not possible/ineffective (IM lorazepam should be refrigerated and diluted per Trust guidance) |
| Repeat | Only after reassessment (mental state, observations, sedation, respiratory rate), usually not sooner than 30–60 minutes |
| Maximum | **2 mg in 24 hours** (senior review required to exceed) |
| Cautions | Respiratory disease/CO₂ retention, sleep apnoea, myasthenia, hepatic impairment, concurrent opioids or other CNS depressants; **paradoxical disinhibition** in older adults; do not give IM within 1 hour of IM olanzapine |
| Reversal | **Flumazenil** must be available where IM/IV benzodiazepines are used (use with caution in chronic benzodiazepine users/epilepsy) |

Diazepam, midazolam and other benzodiazepines should not be used for delirium-related disturbance outside critical care, palliative care or alcohol-withdrawal protocols.

### 12.9 Monitoring after any dose

Following administration of any antipsychotic or benzodiazepine, **monitor and document** (Appendix E):

- respiratory rate
- oxygen saturation
- blood pressure
- pulse
- temperature
- level of consciousness (ACVPU / sedation score)
- degree of agitation/distress (target behaviour)
- adverse effects, **including extrapyramidal symptoms** (dystonia, rigidity, tremor, akathisia), dysphagia, hypotension, oversedation

**Frequency:** at 15–30 minutes after the dose and then at least hourly until there are no further concerns (NICE NG10 standard). Increase to **every 15 minutes** for at least the first hour if: the patient appears asleep or oversedated; the BNF or Trust maximum has been exceeded; IM route was used; the patient has significant physical illness, has had alcohol or illicit drugs, or has been physically restrained. **Repeat observations before any further dose.** Escalate any NEWS2 trigger, respiratory rate < 10, SpO₂ < 90 % (or below target), systolic BP < 90 mmHg, or unresponsiveness immediately. Resuscitation equipment, oxygen, flumazenil and procyclidine must be accessible.

### 12.10 Daily review and stopping

- **The indication for ongoing antipsychotic (or benzodiazepine) treatment should be reviewed at least daily and the drug discontinued as soon as clinically appropriate.** The review should be documented by the responsible medical team and the ward pharmacist.
- **Antipsychotics should not routinely be continued following resolution of delirium.** Continuation of antipsychotics started in hospital for delirium, and their unplanned continuation after discharge, is a recognised patient-safety problem in which it is often unclear who is responsible for review.
- If, exceptionally, an antipsychotic must continue at discharge, the discharge summary must state: the indication; that it was started for delirium; the dose; a **stop date or review date (within 1–2 weeks)**; and **who** will review it (named GP / Care of the Elderly / Older Adult Mental Health / liaison team). See Appendix F.
- Any patient still requiring an antipsychotic for delirium after 7 days must be discussed with Care of the Elderly or Liaison Psychiatry and the diagnosis reconsidered (section 13.6).

### 12.11 Covert administration

- Assess and document the patient's decision-making capacity where practicable. Where a patient lacks capacity, treatment decisions should be made in accordance with the **Mental Capacity Act 2005**, applying **best interests** principles and using the **least restrictive option** (section 14).
- **If covert administration is being considered, this must be undertaken in accordance with the Trust Covert Medication Policy following an appropriate best-interests decision**, involving pharmacy (formulation/interaction with food), the multidisciplinary team and, wherever possible, the family/attorney/IMCA, and recorded in the care plan with a review date. Covert administration is never a routine or convenience measure and should be very rare in acute delirium.

### 12.12 Rapid tranquillisation

Where the level of risk requires parenteral medication for imminent violence rather than the low-dose approach in this section, follow the **Trust Rapid Tranquillisation Policy** (based on NICE NG10), which involves the senior doctor, the RT-competent team and post-RT monitoring. Doses in that policy are **not** appropriate for frail older adults with delirium without senior adjustment; where RT is used in an older adult with delirium, the dose ceilings in this policy (12.4, 12.8) should be applied unless a consultant directs otherwise and documents why.

## 13. Special situations

### 13.1 Parkinson's disease, parkinsonism and dementia with Lewy bodies

- **Do not use haloperidol** or other high-potency D2 antagonists (including metoclopramide and prochlorperazine for nausea).
- Optimise non-pharmacological care; review anticholinergic and dopaminergic drugs with the Parkinson's specialist team (dopaminergic drugs can contribute to delirium but abrupt withdrawal is dangerous – do not stop levodopa; ensure doses are given on time).
- If medication is unavoidable: **quetiapine (12.5–25 mg) after specialist advice**, or low-dose lorazepam. Discuss with Liaison Psychiatry / Neurology / Care of the Elderly.
- In DLB, cholinesterase inhibitors are the specialist treatment for neuropsychiatric symptoms in the longer term – not an acute delirium treatment.

### 13.2 Delirium in people living with dementia

- Manage as delirium first (NICE CG103 1.6.3). Do not attribute a new change to "the dementia".
- Antipsychotics carry an increased risk of stroke and death in people with dementia (MHRA). NICE NG97 allows them only for severe distress or risk of harm, at the lowest dose for the shortest time, reviewed at least every 6 weeks if continued; in delirium the review is **daily** and the drug is stopped on resolution.
- Use the "This is Me" document, carer's passport and dementia-friendly ward measures; involve the dementia nurse specialist **[Trust to confirm]**.

### 13.3 Alcohol and substance withdrawal

- Screen for alcohol dependence on admission (AUDIT-C / clinical history). Delirium tremens typically occurs 48–72 hours after the last drink and is a medical emergency (mortality if untreated).
- Manage under the **Trust Alcohol Withdrawal Guideline** (NICE CG100): symptom-triggered or fixed-schedule **benzodiazepine** (chlordiazepoxide or lorazepam), **parenteral thiamine (Pabrinex)** before glucose, and **oral lorazepam first line for delirium tremens**; parenteral lorazepam, or haloperidol as an adjunct, if oral is refused or symptoms persist. Haloperidol alone is inadequate and lowers the seizure threshold.
- Consider Wernicke's encephalopathy, hypoglycaemia, hepatic encephalopathy, head injury and subdural haematoma in anyone with alcohol misuse and confusion.
- Benzodiazepine, opioid and gabapentinoid withdrawal can also cause delirium – restart or taper rather than stop abruptly.

### 13.4 Critical care and post-operative recovery

- Screen every shift with **CAM-ICU or ICDSC** (NICE CG103 1.6.1). Apply the unit's delirium bundle (e.g. ABCDEF – *Assess pain, Both SAT/SBT, Choice of sedation, Delirium monitoring, Early mobility, Family engagement*).
- Minimise benzodiazepine sedation; dexmedetomidine or propofol under intensivist direction; IV haloperidol only in critical care with continuous ECG monitoring.
- Communicate delirium clearly on step-down to the ward, and re-screen with the 4AT on the ward.

### 13.5 Hip fracture and perioperative care

- People with hip fracture are at very high risk (up to 50 %). Deliver the prevention bundle from ED (NICE CG103; NICE hip fracture guidance; NHS England hip fracture standards specify the 4AT). Early surgery, early orthogeriatric review, adequate analgesia (including fascia iliaca block), avoid anticholinergic antiemetics and excess opioids, early mobilisation.
- Pre-operative assessment should identify at-risk patients for elective surgery and plan prevention; record the pre-operative 4AT as a baseline.

### 13.6 Delirium that does not resolve

If delirium does not resolve within the expected timescale (typically days, though full recovery can take weeks to months): **re-evaluate for underlying causes** not yet identified or treated; **review medication** again; **assess for possible dementia** – but only once delirium has settled sufficiently, and refer to the memory service via the GP rather than diagnosing in hospital (NICE CG103 1.7.6; NG97); consider depression, non-convulsive status, subdural haematoma, encephalitis, hepatic/renal encephalopathy; and discuss with Care of the Elderly / Liaison Psychiatry.

### 13.7 End of life

In the last days of life, delirium is common and the goals of care may change. Continue to look for reversible causes proportionate to prognosis (e.g. retention, constipation, pain, opioid toxicity, hypercalcaemia). Follow the Trust End of Life Care policy and palliative care guidance for terminal agitation (where midazolam and/or haloperidol/levomepromazine may be appropriate on specialist advice). Involve the family and record decisions in an individualised care plan.

### 13.8 Emergency Department and SDEC

- **4AT at triage/initial assessment for all patients aged 65+** and any patient presenting with confusion, falls, reduced mobility or "off legs" **[Trust to confirm; consistent with NHS England and Welsh SDEC standards]**.
- Delirium is a marker of serious illness: prioritise investigation of the cause. Avoid prolonged ED stays, corridor care and multiple moves for people with delirium; involve the frailty team early. Provide hearing aids, glasses, food, drink and a family member at the bedside.
- Medication for behavioural disturbance in ED follows section 12; if the RT policy is invoked, the senior ED doctor is responsible.

### 13.9 Younger adults

Delirium in adults under 65 is usually associated with severe illness, substance use, head injury or a primary neurological cause; the same principles apply. Dose ceilings for haloperidol/lorazepam may be higher in younger, physically robust adults, but always start low, reassess before repeating and record the reasoning for exceeding the older-adult limits.

## 14. Legal and ethical framework

### 14.1 Mental Capacity Act 2005 (England and Wales)

- **Presume capacity**; support the person to make their own decision (glasses, hearing aids, time of day when they are most lucid, plain language, family present).
- Delirium commonly impairs capacity **for specific decisions and for limited periods**. Assess capacity for each significant decision (investigation, treatment, medication, staying in hospital, discharge destination) using the two-stage test, and **document** the assessment. **Reassess as the delirium fluctuates or resolves.**
- If the person lacks capacity, act in their **best interests** (s.4): consider their past and present wishes, feelings, beliefs and values; consult family, carers, any attorney under a Lasting Power of Attorney (health and welfare) or court-appointed deputy; and use an **Independent Mental Capacity Advocate (IMCA)** for serious medical treatment or a change of accommodation where there is no one appropriate to consult.
- Apply the **least restrictive option** in every decision – this is the legal basis for the "oral before IM", "de-escalation before medication", "no restraint unless necessary and proportionate" hierarchy in this policy.
- Check for an **Advance Decision to Refuse Treatment** and for a health-and-welfare LPA.

### 14.2 Restraint (MCA s.5–6)

Restraint (the use or threat of force, or restriction of liberty of movement) of a person who lacks capacity is lawful only if the person doing it **reasonably believes it is necessary to prevent harm to the person** and the act is **a proportionate response to the likelihood and seriousness of that harm**. Chemical restraint (medication given primarily to control behaviour), physical holding, bed rails used to prevent leaving the bed, and "specialing" that prevents free movement are all restraint and must be recorded, justified, time-limited and reviewed under the Trust Restrictive Practice / Restraint Policy, following Restraint Reduction Network principles. Mechanical restraint (e.g. straps, mittens) is not used **[Trust to confirm]**. The Mental Health Units (Use of Force) Act 2018 applies to mental health units; its reporting principles are good practice in the general hospital.

### 14.3 Deprivation of Liberty Safeguards (DoLS) and the Liberty Protection Safeguards (LPS)

- A patient who lacks capacity to consent to their care arrangements, who is **under continuous supervision and control and is not free to leave** (the *Cheshire West* "acid test"), is deprived of their liberty and this must be authorised. In the acute hospital this is currently through **DoLS**: the ward (managing authority) grants an **urgent authorisation (up to 7 days)** and simultaneously applies to the local authority (supervisory body) for a **standard authorisation**. Involve the MCA/DoLS lead. Most patients with delirium who are prevented from leaving, subject to enhanced observation, or given medication to control behaviour will meet the acid test.
- **Status of LPS (at the time of drafting, September 2026):** the Liberty Protection Safeguards (Mental Capacity (Amendment) Act 2019) have **not** been implemented. The Government announced in October 2025 a further consultation on the LPS during 2026, and commentators considered 2027 the earliest realistic commencement. **DoLS therefore remains the operative framework**; under LPS the NHS Trust would become the "Responsible Body" for hospital patients. The Trust MCA lead will update this section when the position changes **[Trust to verify current status before ratification]**.
- Deprivation of liberty for the purpose of treating a mental disorder in a patient who **objects** may fall under the **Mental Health Act 1983** rather than the MCA; delirium is a mental disorder for MHA purposes but the underlying physical cause cannot be treated under the MHA. Seek Liaison Psychiatry advice where a patient with delirium is actively resisting and DoLS may be inappropriate.

### 14.4 Scotland and Northern Ireland

For Trust services operating outside England, the Adults with Incapacity (Scotland) Act 2000 (section 47 certificate) and the Mental Capacity Act (Northern Ireland) 2016 apply respectively **[delete if not applicable]**.

### 14.5 Safeguarding

Delirium can be the presentation of neglect, abuse, self-neglect or medication error. Follow the Safeguarding Adults Policy where there are concerns. Consider a Datix/incident report for any use of restraint, any rapid tranquillisation, any prescribing outside this policy, and any delirium-associated serious harm (fall with injury, absconding, aspiration).

### 14.6 Consent, information and confidentiality

Share information with families and carers in the patient's best interests where they lack capacity, and with the patient's consent where they have it. Record who has been informed and what was said.

## 15. Information and support for patients, families and carers

(NICE CG103 1.8.1; NICE QS63 statement 4; SIGN 157)

**Offer information – verbally and in writing, in an accessible and culturally appropriate format – to the person with (or at risk of) delirium and to their family/carers** that:

- explains that delirium is **common and usually temporary**, and what has caused it in their case;
- describes **what people experience** during delirium (frightening hallucinations, feeling trapped, believing staff are trying to harm them) so that families understand behaviour is part of the illness;
- **encourages families to tell staff about any sudden change or fluctuation** in behaviour – "you know them best";
- explains how they can help: **visit often, stay at the bedside where possible, bring glasses, hearing aids, dentures, familiar objects and photographs; keep a gentle, friendly approach; talk in short simple sentences; keep them informed; help with meals and drinks**;
- explains that **recovery can take weeks or months** and that the person may not be entirely themselves at discharge, may be less able to manage daily activities, and may have persisting memory gaps;
- encourages the person, **after recovery**, to talk about their experience with a healthcare professional;
- gives details of **support groups and sources of further information** (e.g. Alzheimer's Society, Dementia UK, Age UK, Royal College of Psychiatrists patient information; local carers' services) **[Trust to attach leaflet – e.g. RCPsych "Delirium" leaflet or Trust leaflet]**.

Where a person lacks capacity, families and carers should be involved in **best-interests decisions**, including decisions about medication and about restrictions. Apply open visiting for carers of people with delirium and provide a carer's passport **[Trust to confirm]**.

## 16. Discharge, communication with primary care and follow-up

(NICE CG103 1.6.5; NICE QS63 statement 5; SIGN 157 good practice points)

1. **Do not discharge until delirium is resolving and the destination is safe.** A person still in delirium is at high risk of readmission, falls and failed discharge. Where discharge with persisting delirium is planned (e.g. to a care home with support), this must be an MDT best-interests decision with a clear follow-up plan.
2. **The diagnosis of delirium (current or resolved) must be stated explicitly in the discharge summary**, with the identified cause(s), and coded (SNOMED CT "Delirium") in the hospital record so it appears in the primary care record and future risk assessments.
3. **Medication changes** must be explicit: any antipsychotic or benzodiazepine started for delirium should normally be stopped before discharge; if not, state indication, dose, **stop/review date and named reviewer** (12.10). List culprit drugs stopped or reduced and whether they should be restarted.
4. **Request GP review** within 4–6 weeks; and **cognitive and functional assessment** once recovered (SIGN 157) – typically at 8–12 weeks – with referral to the memory service if impairment persists (NICE NG97). Do not label the person with dementia during or immediately after delirium.
5. **Inform the care home / community team** verbally and in writing of the delirium, its triggers and the prevention measures that helped; provide the family with the written information in section 15 and discuss whether they need extra support.
6. **Future risk**: flag "previous delirium" on the record; advise the patient/family that they are at higher risk with future illness or surgery and should mention it at pre-operative assessment.

See Appendix F.

## Appendix A: 4AT – items and scoring

*(Reproduced in summary from the4at.com, CC BY 4.0. Use the official form for scoring.)*

| Item | How to assess | Score |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Alertness** | Observe. Normal (fully alert, not agitated) = 0. Mild sleepiness < 10 s after waking, then normal = 0. Clearly abnormal (drowsy, difficult to rouse, or agitated/hyperactive) = **4** | 0 or 4 |
| **2. AMT4** | Age, date of birth, place (name of hospital/building), current year. No mistakes = 0; 1 mistake = 1; ≥ 2 mistakes or untestable = 2 | 0 / 1 / 2 |
| **3. Attention** | "Please tell me the months of the year in backwards order, starting at December." Achieves ≥ 7 months correctly = 0; starts but scores < 7 months or refuses to start = 1; untestable (cannot start because unwell, drowsy, inattentive) = 2 | 0 / 1 / 2 |
| **4. Acute change or fluctuating course** | Evidence of significant change or fluctuation in alertness, cognition or other mental function (e.g. paranoia, hallucinations) arising over the last 2 weeks and still evident in the last 24 hours. No = 0; Yes = **4** | 0 or 4 |

**Total (0–12):** **≥ 4 – possible delirium ± cognitive impairment**; **1–3 – possible cognitive impairment**; **0 – delirium or severe cognitive impairment unlikely (but delirium still possible if item 4 information incomplete)**.

Worked example (from Trust teaching): a patient who is drowsy, disorientated to time and place, and fluctuating scores 4 (alertness) + 2 (AMT4) + 2 (attention, untestable) + 4 (fluctuation) = **12 → delirium positive**.

## Appendix C: Delirium vs dementia – quick comparison

| Feature | Delirium | Dementia |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | Acute (hours–days) | Insidious (months–years) |
| Course | Fluctuates, often worse at night | Steady, progressive decline (sundowning may occur in Alzheimer's) |
| Duration | Days–weeks (can be months) | Years |
| Attention | **Impaired early** – the cardinal feature | Preserved in early stages |
| Level of consciousness | Altered (drowsy or hyper-alert) | Normal until late |
| Hallucinations | Common, especially visual | Less common (except DLB) |
| Sleep–wake cycle | Disrupted | Usually intact early |
| Speech | Incoherent, rambling | Word-finding difficulty, aphasia |
| Reversibility | Often reversible if cause treated | Largely irreversible |
| Underlying cause | Medical illness, drugs, withdrawal | Neurodegenerative / vascular |
| Typical tools | 4AT, CAM-ICU | AMTS, MoCA, ACE-III (after delirium resolved) |
| **If unsure** | **Manage as delirium first** | |

Common dementia subtypes and clues (Trust teaching): **Alzheimer's** – memory loss first, hippocampal atrophy; **Dementia with Lewy bodies** – visual hallucinations, fluctuating cognition, parkinsonism, REM sleep behaviour disorder (**avoid haloperidol**); **Frontotemporal** – personality and behavioural change; **Vascular** – stepwise deterioration after strokes.

## Appendix D: Pharmacological management – one-page quick reference

```
SEVERE BEHAVIOURAL DISTURBANCE IN DELIRIUM – QUICK REFERENCE
===============================================================
STEP 1  Is there an unmet need / acute physical cause?  Pain · retention · constipation ·
        hypoxia · hypoglycaemia · withdrawal · fear.   → TREAT IT.
STEP 2  De-escalate: calm, one voice, reorientate, reduce stimulation, family, distract.
        Document attempts.  Enhanced observation. Falls plan.
STEP 3  Medication ONLY if de-escalation ineffective/not feasible AND patient is:
          • severely distressed; or
          • an immediate risk of physical harm to self/others; or
          • preventing essential investigation/treatment that cannot safely be delayed.
        NOT for: falls risk, wandering, calling out, night restlessness, hypoactive delirium.
STEP 4  Check: Parkinson's / DLB / parkinsonism?  ──YES──►  NO HALOPERIDOL.
                                                            Quetiapine 12.5–25 mg (specialist advice)
                                                            or lorazepam 0.5 mg. Senior review.
        Check: ECG / QTc  (>450 M, >460 F = prolonged; >500 = do not give haloperidol)
        Correct K+, Mg2+, Ca2+.  Review other QT-prolonging drugs.  Previous NMS? Recent MI? Arrhythmia?
        Capacity assessed & documented; best-interests decision if lacking capacity.
        Do not delay for ECG if IMMEDIATE serious risk – document and obtain ASAP.
STEP 5  HALOPERIDOL  0.25–0.5 mg ORAL (IM only if oral impossible/ineffective)
        Repeat ONLY after reassessment of mental state, obs, adverse effects – usually 30–60 min.
        ≥65 / frail: cumulative >2 mg/24 h needs senior (SpR/consultant) review.
        Usually ≤1 week.  Review indication DAILY.  >48 h anticipated → CoE consultant / Liaison Psychiatry.
   or   LORAZEPAM 0.5 mg PO/IM (oral first) – only for alcohol/benzo withdrawal, specialist advice,
        or where antipsychotics contraindicated/high risk after senior review.  Max 2 mg/24 h.
        Never IM lorazepam within 1 h of IM olanzapine.  Flumazenil available.
STEP 6  MONITOR & DOCUMENT: RR · SpO2 · BP · pulse · temp · consciousness (ACVPU) ·
        agitation/distress · adverse effects incl. EPS.  15–30 min post dose then hourly
        until no concerns (q15 min if asleep/oversedated, IM, max exceeded, restrained).
        REPEAT OBS BEFORE ANY FURTHER DOSE.  Procyclidine available for dystonia.
STEP 7  REVIEW DAILY.  STOP as soon as clinically appropriate.  Do NOT continue at
        discharge unless exceptional – then stop/review date + named reviewer.
        Covert medication → Trust Covert Medication Policy + best-interests decision only.
        Restraint / deprivation of liberty → MCA s.6 proportionality; DoLS urgent authorisation.
```

## Appendix F: Discharge checklist after delirium

- [ ] Delirium (current/resolved) stated in the discharge summary and coded
- [ ] Cause(s) identified and stated; any outstanding investigations listed
- [ ] Antipsychotic/benzodiazepine started for delirium **stopped** – or, if continuing: indication, dose, stop/review date, named reviewer stated
- [ ] Culprit medicines stopped/reduced listed, with advice on whether/when to restart
- [ ] GP review requested (4–6 weeks)
- [ ] Cognitive and functional assessment after recovery requested (8–12 weeks); memory service referral if impairment persists
- [ ] Care home / community team informed verbally and in writing of triggers and helpful measures
- [ ] Patient and family given written delirium information and told recovery may take weeks–months
- [ ] Family/carer support needs discussed
- [ ] "Previous delirium" flag added; patient/family advised to mention at future admissions/pre-op assessment
- [ ] Capacity/DoLS status at discharge documented; DoLS authorisation ended or transferred as appropriate
