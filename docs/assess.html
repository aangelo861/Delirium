Task

Assess suspected delirium

Immediate action: Immediate safety assessment

An acute change in cognition is a medical emergency until proven otherwise. Before or alongside cognitive testing, complete an ABCDE assessment: observations (NEWS2), capillary glucose, oxygen saturation, pain assessment, bladder scan for retention, bowel chart, medication chart review and a drug/alcohol history (Trust teaching; SIGN 157 – first consider acute life-threatening causes: hypoxia, hypotension, hypoglycaemia, intoxication or withdrawal).

Important: Quiet, withdrawn or drowsy patients

Be particularly vigilant for the indicators of hypoactive delirium – withdrawal, slow responses, reduced mobility, reduced oral intake, drowsiness – which are often missed. "They're just tired / sleepy / a bit low" should prompt a 4AT.

Choose the assessment tool by care setting

Wards, ED, SDEC and long-term care

Use the 4AT in general hospital wards, the Emergency Department, SDEC and long-term care. It takes under 2 minutes, needs no special training, is free to use, and includes brief cognitive testing (Appendix A).

Critical care or post-operative recovery

In critical care or post-operative recovery, use the CAM-ICU or the Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist (ICDSC) instead (CG103 1.6.1).

Pathway

  1. New or fluctuating change

    Include quiet, withdrawn or drowsy presentations. Any change reported by a carer, relative or staff member must be documented and acted on (section 8.2).

  2. Immediate safety assessment

    ABCDE, observations (NEWS2) and capillary glucose, before or alongside cognitive testing (section 9.1).

  3. Which care setting?

    • Critical care or post-operative recovery

      CAM-ICU or ICDSC

    • Other settings within this policy

      4AT – open the official 4AT form

  4. Read the result with two cautions

    Interpretation: ≥ 4 = probable delirium (± cognitive impairment); 1–3 = possible cognitive impairment, delirium not excluded; 0 = delirium unlikely (but does not exclude it if the history is suggestive).

    Caution:

    The 4AT supports but does not replace clinical judgement.

    If it is difficult to distinguish delirium, dementia or delirium superimposed on dementia, manage as delirium first.

  5. Clinical assessment and collateral baseline

    A healthcare professional trained and competent in diagnosing delirium should carry out (or confirm) the clinical assessment and make the final diagnosis; this may be the same person who did the 4AT.

    Document the diagnosis of delirium clearly in the medical record (problem list) and ensure it appears in the discharge summary to primary care (section 16).

Using the 4AT
Open the official 4AT form (the4at.com)
  • Use the 4AT in general hospital wards, the Emergency Department, SDEC and long-term care. It takes under 2 minutes, needs no special training, is free to use, and includes brief cognitive testing (Appendix A).
  • In critical care or post-operative recovery, use the CAM-ICU or the Intensive Care Delirium Screening Checklist (ICDSC) instead (CG103 1.6.1).
  • Interpretation: ≥ 4 = probable delirium (± cognitive impairment); 1–3 = possible cognitive impairment, delirium not excluded; 0 = delirium unlikely (but does not exclude it if the history is suggestive).
  • Record the score and date/time in the notes and on the electronic record [Trust to confirm EPR field].

Worked example (from Trust teaching): a patient who is drowsy, disorientated to time and place, and fluctuating scores 4 (alertness) + 2 (AMT4) + 2 (attention, untestable) + 4 (fluctuation) = 12 → delirium positive.

Appendix A: 4AT – items and scoring

When to do a 4AT
  • Admission of any at-risk patient (section 6)
  • NEWS2 "C – new confusion" (scores 3; triggers urgent clinical review)
  • Any of the indicators in 8.1
  • Fall, new incontinence, refusal of food/fluids/medication, new drowsiness
  • Post-operatively (recovery and day 1–3), and on transfer from critical care
  • A family member saying "this is not like them"
  • Any behavioural incident, before medication is considered

Indicators at presentation

Assess for any recent (hours to days) change or fluctuation in behaviour, reported by the patient, carer or relative, including:

  • Cognitive function – worsened concentration, slow responses, confusion, disorientation
  • Perception – visual or auditory hallucinations, misinterpretations, paranoid ideas
  • Physical function – reduced mobility, reduced movement, restlessness, agitation, changes in appetite, sleep disturbance
  • Social behaviour – lack of co-operation with reasonable requests, withdrawal, altered communication, mood or attitude

Be particularly vigilant for the indicators of hypoactive delirium – withdrawal, slow responses, reduced mobility, reduced oral intake, drowsiness – which are often missed. "They're just tired / sleepy / a bit low" should prompt a 4AT.

Daily observation

Observe at least daily all people in hospital or long-term care for recent changes or fluctuations in usual behaviour. Any change reported by a carer, relative or staff member must be documented and acted on – complete a 4AT and inform the responsible clinician.

Making the diagnosis
  • A healthcare professional trained and competent in diagnosing delirium should carry out (or confirm) the clinical assessment and make the final diagnosis; this may be the same person who did the 4AT.
  • The diagnosis is clinical: acute onset and fluctuating course + inattention + altered level of consciousness or disorganised thinking (the CAM/DSM-5 construct). The 4AT supports but does not replace clinical judgement.
  • If it is difficult to distinguish delirium, dementia or delirium superimposed on dementia, manage as delirium first.
  • Document the diagnosis of delirium clearly in the medical record (problem list) and ensure it appears in the discharge summary to primary care (section 16).

Collateral history and baseline

Establish the person's baseline cognition and function from a relative, carer, care home, GP record or "This is Me" / carer's passport document. Key questions (Trust teaching): When did the change start? What is normal for them? Has anything changed – medicines, bowels, sleep, intake, environment?

Delirium or dementia?

Delirium is acute (hours–days), fluctuating, with impaired attention early, altered arousal and frequent hallucinations, and is often reversible. Dementia is chronic (months–years), progressive, with preserved attention early, and is largely irreversible. See Appendix C. Structured tools such as the long CAM or the Observational Scale of Level of Arousal (OSLA) may help, but a new diagnosis of dementia should not be made during an episode of delirium (NICE NG97). Dementia with Lewy bodies (fluctuation, visual hallucinations, parkinsonism, REM sleep behaviour disorder) can mimic delirium – and is the single most important diagnosis to identify before any antipsychotic is considered.

Common dementia subtypes and clues (Trust teaching): Alzheimer's – memory loss first, hippocampal atrophy; Dementia with Lewy bodies – visual hallucinations, fluctuating cognition, parkinsonism, REM sleep behaviour disorder (avoid haloperidol); Frontotemporal – personality and behavioural change; Vascular – stepwise deterioration after strokes.

Risk assessment

Document and plan for specific risks (Trust teaching): wandering/absconding; falls; self-harm; aggression towards staff or other patients; removal of cannulas, feeding tubes, catheters, oxygen; sexual disinhibition; damage to property; refusal of essential treatment; dehydration/malnutrition (hypoactive). Complete the falls risk assessment and consider enhanced observation / cohort nursing before medication.

Mental capacity

Assess and document decision-specific capacity for key decisions (investigations, treatment, remaining in hospital) as soon as practicable. Capacity fluctuates in delirium and must be reassessed. See section 14.
