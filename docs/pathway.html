    Assess suspected delirium

    Probable delirium – confirm, document, then treat the cause

    Urgent: Probable delirium – confirm, document, then treat the cause

    Interpretation: ≥ 4 = probable delirium (± cognitive impairment); 1–3 = possible cognitive impairment, delirium not excluded; 0 = delirium unlikely (but does not exclude it if the history is suggestive).

    • A healthcare professional trained and competent in diagnosing delirium should carry out (or confirm) the clinical assessment and make the final diagnosis; this may be the same person who did the 4AT.
    • The diagnosis is clinical: acute onset and fluctuating course + inattention + altered level of consciousness or disorganised thinking (the CAM/DSM-5 construct). The 4AT supports but does not replace clinical judgement.
    • If it is difficult to distinguish delirium, dementia or delirium superimposed on dementia, manage as delirium first.
    • Document the diagnosis of delirium clearly in the medical record (problem list) and ensure it appears in the discharge summary to primary care (section 16).

    Manage severe distress

    Unmet needs and de-escalation come first

    Guidance: Unmet needs and de-escalation come first

    If a person with delirium is distressed or considered a risk to themselves or others, staff must first use verbal and non-verbal de-escalation techniques:

    1. Look for an unmet need or a physical cause – pain, thirst, hunger, full bladder/bowels, cold/hot, fear, hypoxia, hypoglycaemia, a new acute problem.
    2. Approach calmly from the front, at eye level, one person talking, use their name, a soft voice, open body language, no sudden movements.
    3. Reduce stimulation – noise, crowding, alarms, bright lights; remove the audience.
    4. Reorientate and reassure; offer choices; involve a family member if available (by telephone/video if not).
    5. Distract and redirect – a drink, a walk, an activity.
    6. Do not confront, restrain, or "correct" delusional beliefs.
    7. Give space and time; re-approach later if safe to do so.

    Document what was tried and the response. Only if these are ineffective or inappropriate (e.g. immediate serious risk) should medication be considered (section 12) – this is the standard set by NICE QS63 statement 3.

    Manage severe distress

    Do not use haloperidol

    Urgent: Do not use haloperidol

    • Do not use haloperidol or other high-potency D2 antagonists (including metoclopramide and prochlorperazine for nausea).
    • Optimise non-pharmacological care; review anticholinergic and dopaminergic drugs with the Parkinson's specialist team (dopaminergic drugs can contribute to delirium but abrupt withdrawal is dangerous – do not stop levodopa; ensure doses are given on time).
    • If medication is unavoidable: quetiapine (12.5–25 mg) after specialist advice, or low-dose lorazepam. Discuss with Liaison Psychiatry / Neurology / Care of the Elderly.
    • In DLB, cholinesterase inhibitors are the specialist treatment for neuropsychiatric symptoms in the longer term – not an acute delirium treatment.

    Haloperidol contraindicated or high risk – senior or specialist advice

    Urgent: Haloperidol contraindicated or high risk – senior or specialist advice

    If the QTc is prolonged or there is concern regarding arrhythmia risk, seek senior or specialist advice and consider an alternative agent.

    NICE doesn't recommend one antipsychotic over another. Choice should be tailored to comorbidities, side-effect profile and available routes. Seek specialist advice from Liaison Psychiatry regarding alternative medication where haloperidol is contraindicated. Indicative starting doses below are for older/frail adults and must be confirmed against the BNF/Maudsley with pharmacy [Trust to confirm doses]:

    In exceptional circumstances, where severe agitation presents an immediate risk and antipsychotics are contraindicated or considered high risk (e.g. Parkinson's disease/DLB, QTc > 500 ms, previous NMS, recent MI), an individualised decision to use a benzodiazepine may be appropriate following senior clinical review. Pragmatism and patient-tailored choice should carry weight: for example, if urgent treatment of severe psychomotor agitation is required in a patient with diabetes, significant cardiac history and parkinsonism, a benzodiazepine may be the safer choice for that person in that moment. The choice of drug category, and of drug within that category, should be tailored to the patient's history and acuity.

    Manage severe distress

    Manage under the alcohol withdrawal guideline

    Urgent: Manage under the alcohol withdrawal guideline

    • Screen for alcohol dependence on admission (AUDIT-C / clinical history). Delirium tremens typically occurs 48–72 hours after the last drink and is a medical emergency (mortality if untreated).
    • Manage under the Trust Alcohol Withdrawal Guideline (NICE CG100): symptom-triggered or fixed-schedule benzodiazepine (chlordiazepoxide or lorazepam), parenteral thiamine (Pabrinex) before glucose, and oral lorazepam first line for delirium tremens; parenteral lorazepam, or haloperidol as an adjunct, if oral is refused or symptoms persist. Haloperidol alone is inadequate and lowers the seizure threshold.
    • Consider Wernicke's encephalopathy, hypoglycaemia, hepatic encephalopathy, head injury and subdural haematoma in anyone with alcohol misuse and confusion.
    • Benzodiazepine, opioid and gabapentinoid withdrawal can also cause delirium – restart or taper rather than stop abruptly.

    Manage severe distress

    Haloperidol may be considered – complete the safety checks first

    Important: Haloperidol may be considered – complete the safety checks first

    • Non-pharmacological measures and de-escalation tried and documented (or reason why not feasible)
    • Reversible causes sought (pain, retention, constipation, hypoxia, hypoglycaemia, withdrawal)
    • Parkinson's disease, dementia with Lewy bodies, parkinsonism – excluded (if present → do not use haloperidol; see 12.5)
    • Recent ECG reviewed or performed where feasible; QTc noted; electrolytes (K⁺, Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺) checked and corrected (12.6)
    • Other QT-prolonging drugs identified (e.g. citalopram/escitalopram, macrolides, quinolones, ondansetron, methadone, amiodarone, domperidone, tricyclics, other antipsychotics)
    • Capacity assessed and documented; best-interests decision recorded if the patient lacks capacity (section 14)
    • Route – oral offered first
    • Indication and target behaviour documented in the notes and on the prescription
    • Monitoring plan in place (12.9)

    Then use the haloperidol reference card. Oral first, lowest clinically appropriate dose.

    Repeat dosing
    Repeat only after reassessment of mental state, physical observations and adverse effects – usually after 30–60 minutes if clinically indicated. Do not repeat "until sedation achieved". The Maudsley guidelines are cautious about repeated dosing in frail older adults.
    Cumulative 24-hour limits and senior review
    For older adults (65+) or frail patients: avoid cumulative doses above 2 mg in 24 hours without senior review (registrar or consultant). For younger, non-frail adults, doses above 2 mg/24 h also require senior review; the Trust does not endorse routine use above 5 mg/24 h [Trust to confirm].

    Monitor after medication

    Daily review and stopping

    Guidance: Daily review and stopping

    • The indication for ongoing antipsychotic (or benzodiazepine) treatment should be reviewed at least daily and the drug discontinued as soon as clinically appropriate. The review should be documented by the responsible medical team and the ward pharmacist.
    • Antipsychotics should not routinely be continued following resolution of delirium. Continuation of antipsychotics started in hospital for delirium, and their unplanned continuation after discharge, is a recognised patient-safety problem in which it is often unclear who is responsible for review.
    • If, exceptionally, an antipsychotic must continue at discharge, the discharge summary must state: the indication; that it was started for delirium; the dose; a stop date or review date (within 1–2 weeks); and who will review it (named GP / Care of the Elderly / Older Adult Mental Health / liaison team). See Appendix F.
    • Any patient still requiring an antipsychotic for delirium after 7 days must be discussed with Care of the Elderly or Liaison Psychiatry and the diagnosis reconsidered (section 13.6).

    Discharge and follow-up

    Discharge summary, follow-up and family information

    Guidance: Discharge summary, follow-up and family information

    1. The diagnosis of delirium (current or resolved) must be stated explicitly in the discharge summary, with the identified cause(s), and coded (SNOMED CT "Delirium") in the hospital record so it appears in the primary care record and future risk assessments.
    2. Request GP review within 4–6 weeks; and cognitive and functional assessment once recovered (SIGN 157) – typically at 8–12 weeks – with referral to the memory service if impairment persists (NICE NG97). Do not label the person with dementia during or immediately after delirium.
    3. Inform the care home / community team verbally and in writing of the delirium, its triggers and the prevention measures that helped; provide the family with the written information in section 15 and discuss whether they need extra support.
    4. Future risk: flag "previous delirium" on the record; advise the patient/family that they are at higher risk with future illness or surgery and should mention it at pre-operative assessment.

    Special situations and capacity

    Parkinson's disease, parkinsonism and dementia with Lewy bodies

    Urgent: Parkinson's disease, parkinsonism and dementia with Lewy bodies

    • Do not use haloperidol or other high-potency D2 antagonists (including metoclopramide and prochlorperazine for nausea).
    • Optimise non-pharmacological care; review anticholinergic and dopaminergic drugs with the Parkinson's specialist team (dopaminergic drugs can contribute to delirium but abrupt withdrawal is dangerous – do not stop levodopa; ensure doses are given on time).
    • If medication is unavoidable: quetiapine (12.5–25 mg) after specialist advice, or low-dose lorazepam. Discuss with Liaison Psychiatry / Neurology / Care of the Elderly.
    • In DLB, cholinesterase inhibitors are the specialist treatment for neuropsychiatric symptoms in the longer term – not an acute delirium treatment.

    Delirium in people living with dementia

    Guidance: Delirium in people living with dementia

    • Manage as delirium first (NICE CG103 1.6.3). Do not attribute a new change to "the dementia".
    • Antipsychotics carry an increased risk of stroke and death in people with dementia (MHRA). NICE NG97 allows them only for severe distress or risk of harm, at the lowest dose for the shortest time, reviewed at least every 6 weeks if continued; in delirium the review is daily and the drug is stopped on resolution.
    • Use the "This is Me" document, carer's passport and dementia-friendly ward measures; involve the dementia nurse specialist [Trust to confirm].

    Special situations and capacity

    Alcohol and substance withdrawal

    Urgent: Alcohol and substance withdrawal

    • Screen for alcohol dependence on admission (AUDIT-C / clinical history). Delirium tremens typically occurs 48–72 hours after the last drink and is a medical emergency (mortality if untreated).
    • Manage under the Trust Alcohol Withdrawal Guideline (NICE CG100): symptom-triggered or fixed-schedule benzodiazepine (chlordiazepoxide or lorazepam), parenteral thiamine (Pabrinex) before glucose, and oral lorazepam first line for delirium tremens; parenteral lorazepam, or haloperidol as an adjunct, if oral is refused or symptoms persist. Haloperidol alone is inadequate and lowers the seizure threshold.
    • Consider Wernicke's encephalopathy, hypoglycaemia, hepatic encephalopathy, head injury and subdural haematoma in anyone with alcohol misuse and confusion.
    • Benzodiazepine, opioid and gabapentinoid withdrawal can also cause delirium – restart or taper rather than stop abruptly.

    Critical care and post-operative recovery

    Guidance: Critical care and post-operative recovery

    • Screen every shift with CAM-ICU or ICDSC (NICE CG103 1.6.1). Apply the unit's delirium bundle (e.g. ABCDEF – Assess pain, Both SAT/SBT, Choice of sedation, Delirium monitoring, Early mobility, Family engagement).
    • Minimise benzodiazepine sedation; dexmedetomidine or propofol under intensivist direction; IV haloperidol only in critical care with continuous ECG monitoring.
    • Communicate delirium clearly on step-down to the ward, and re-screen with the 4AT on the ward.

    Hip fracture and perioperative care

    Guidance: Hip fracture and perioperative care

    • People with hip fracture are at very high risk (up to 50 %). Deliver the prevention bundle from ED (NICE CG103; NICE hip fracture guidance; NHS England hip fracture standards specify the 4AT). Early surgery, early orthogeriatric review, adequate analgesia (including fascia iliaca block), avoid anticholinergic antiemetics and excess opioids, early mobilisation.
    • Pre-operative assessment should identify at-risk patients for elective surgery and plan prevention; record the pre-operative 4AT as a baseline.

    Delirium that does not resolve

    Important: Delirium that does not resolve

    If delirium does not resolve within the expected timescale (typically days, though full recovery can take weeks to months): re-evaluate for underlying causes not yet identified or treated; review medication again; assess for possible dementia – but only once delirium has settled sufficiently, and refer to the memory service via the GP rather than diagnosing in hospital (NICE CG103 1.7.6; NG97); consider depression, non-convulsive status, subdural haematoma, encephalitis, hepatic/renal encephalopathy; and discuss with Care of the Elderly / Liaison Psychiatry.

    End of life

    Guidance: End of life

    In the last days of life, delirium is common and the goals of care may change. Continue to look for reversible causes proportionate to prognosis (e.g. retention, constipation, pain, opioid toxicity, hypercalcaemia). Follow the Trust End of Life Care policy and palliative care guidance for terminal agitation (where midazolam and/or haloperidol/levomepromazine may be appropriate on specialist advice). Involve the family and record decisions in an individualised care plan.

    Emergency Department and SDEC

    Guidance: Emergency Department and SDEC

    • 4AT at triage/initial assessment for all patients aged 65+ and any patient presenting with confusion, falls, reduced mobility or "off legs" [Trust to confirm; consistent with NHS England and Welsh SDEC standards].
    • Delirium is a marker of serious illness: prioritise investigation of the cause. Avoid prolonged ED stays, corridor care and multiple moves for people with delirium; involve the frailty team early. Provide hearing aids, glasses, food, drink and a family member at the bedside.
    • Medication for behavioural disturbance in ED follows section 12; if the RT policy is invoked, the senior ED doctor is responsible.

    Younger adults

    Guidance: Younger adults

    Delirium in adults under 65 is usually associated with severe illness, substance use, head injury or a primary neurological cause; the same principles apply. Dose ceilings for haloperidol/lorazepam may be higher in younger, physically robust adults, but always start low, reassess before repeating and record the reasoning for exceeding the older-adult limits.

    Mental capacity, restraint and deprivation of liberty

    Guidance: Mental capacity, restraint and deprivation of liberty

    • Presume capacity; support the person to make their own decision (glasses, hearing aids, time of day when they are most lucid, plain language, family present).
    • Delirium commonly impairs capacity for specific decisions and for limited periods. Assess capacity for each significant decision (investigation, treatment, medication, staying in hospital, discharge destination) using the two-stage test, and document the assessment. Reassess as the delirium fluctuates or resolves.
    • If the person lacks capacity, act in their best interests (s.4): consider their past and present wishes, feelings, beliefs and values; consult family, carers, any attorney under a Lasting Power of Attorney (health and welfare) or court-appointed deputy; and use an Independent Mental Capacity Advocate (IMCA) for serious medical treatment or a change of accommodation where there is no one appropriate to consult.
    • Apply the least restrictive option in every decision – this is the legal basis for the "oral before IM", "de-escalation before medication", "no restraint unless necessary and proportionate" hierarchy in this policy.
    • Check for an Advance Decision to Refuse Treatment and for a health-and-welfare LPA.
